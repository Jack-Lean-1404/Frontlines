Air Combat

Air Combat governs engagements between aircraft and the specialised systems that support aerial warfare. Aircraft operate across large areas of the battlefield and must manage operational range, contested airspace, enemy aircraft, and ground-based air defences.

Successful air operations depend on more than aircraft strength. Commanders must consider operational anchors, aircraft range, air defence coverage, formation size, and supporting assets when planning aerial operations.

Air Operations

Aircraft operate from friendly airports or suitable aerial anchor points. Each aircraft has a defined movement range that determines how far it may operate from its anchor point.

Airports provide the primary fixed bases for aircraft operations. Tankers can also function as mobile anchor points, extending the operational area available to friendly aircraft.

Air Operations Learn about aircraft range, airports, tankers, and operational anchor points.

Operational Range

Aircraft may only operate within their defined movement range from a friendly airport or other valid aerial anchor point.

Different aircraft have different operational limits. For example, Multi-Role Fighters have a range of 4 sectors from an anchor point, while Bombers have a range of 10 sectors. Helicopters follow their own defined range limits.

Strategic Airlift Aircraft have an operational range of 10 sectors from an airport or tanker.

Aircraft cannot operate beyond their maximum range from a valid anchor point.

Tankers

Tankers function as mobile aerial anchor points. They allow friendly aircraft to operate farther from fixed airports while remaining within their operational limits.

Tankers themselves must operate from an airport and cannot use another tanker as their operational anchor. Helicopters are subject to the same restriction and must use an airport as their base of operations.

This prevents chains of mobile anchor points from extending aircraft operations indefinitely.

Loss of an Anchor Point

Airports and tankers are critical to maintaining aerial operations. If a friendly airport or tanker is destroyed, aircraft relying on that anchor point must determine whether another valid anchor point remains within range.

Aircraft that cannot reach another friendly airport or tanker after the destruction of their operational anchor point are lost and removed from play.

Protecting airports and tankers is therefore an important part of maintaining long-range air operations.

Contested Airspace

Aircraft may encounter enemy air units when entering sectors containing hostile aircraft.

When entering a contested sector, an air unit may either engage the enemy or bypass the sector.

Bypassing a contested sector costs 2 Movement Points. Engaging the enemy costs 1 Movement Point and represents direct combat with hostile air units occupying the sector.

The choice between engagement and bypassing allows commanders to decide whether to commit aircraft to aerial combat or continue toward their operational objective.

Aircraft Damage

Aircraft use formation size to represent the overall strength of an aerial formation.

When an air unit is hit, its formation size is immediately reduced by one level.

For example, a formation may be reduced from Wing to Squadron, or from Squadron to Flight. Reducing the formation represents aircraft losses and a corresponding reduction in the unit's combat effectiveness.

Aircraft Destruction

If an air unit is already at its smallest formation size and is hit again, the unit is permanently destroyed and removed from play.

This destruction rule applies regardless of the source of the damage.

Anti-Air Defence

Anti-Air units provide ground-based protection against hostile aircraft. They establish Air Defence Zones around their position and automatically engage enemy aircraft that enter or operate within their coverage.

An Anti-Air unit controls a one-sector radius around itself. Any enemy air unit entering or operating within this area is automatically engaged by the Anti-Air unit.

Anti-Air Combat

When an Anti-Air unit engages an aircraft, the aircraft must resolve an evasion check based on its classification.

Non-stealth aircraft have an 80% chance of being hit by Anti-Air fire.

Stealth aircraft have a 20% chance of being hit, reflecting their reduced detectability and advanced countermeasures.

If the Anti-Air attack succeeds, the targeted aircraft loses one formation size. If the attack fails, no damage is dealt.

Suppression of Enemy Air Defences

SEAD, or Suppression of Enemy Air Defences, is a specialised aerial operation used to neutralise enemy Anti-Air units and reduce the effectiveness of their Air Defence Zones.

When an aircraft conducts a SEAD mission against an Anti-Air unit, the Anti-Air unit fires first and attempts to destroy the attacking aircraft.

If the attacking aircraft survives, it has a flat 60% chance of destroying the targeted Anti-Air unit.

The SEAD destruction chance is not affected by the strength or defence value of either the attacking aircraft or the targeted Anti-Air unit.

Stealth aircraft are particularly effective at conducting SEAD missions because they have only a 20% chance of being hit by Anti-Air fire.

AWACS

AWACS aircraft provide airborne warning and control capabilities that enhance the effectiveness of friendly aerial operations.

An AWACS unit supports all friendly air units within a two-sector radius.

Friendly aircraft within this coverage receive a 50% increase to both Strength and Defence.

AWACS bonuses do not stack. If multiple AWACS units cover the same aircraft, only one AWACS bonus is applied.

AWACS aircraft are high-value assets and should be protected from enemy attacks. Their loss can significantly reduce the effectiveness of friendly air operations.

Stealth Aircraft

Stealth aircraft are specialised aerial assets designed to operate with a reduced probability of detection and engagement by enemy air defence systems.

Stealth aircraft are twice as effective as non-stealth aircraft in terms of both Strength and Defence.

Their reduced detectability also makes them significantly more resistant to Anti-Air systems.

Anti-Air attacks against stealth aircraft have only a 20% chance of successfully hitting the aircraft, compared with an 80% chance against non-stealth aircraft.

Strategic Airlift

Strategic Airlift Aircraft provide rapid deployment of ground forces across long distances.

A single Strategic Airlift Aircraft can transport any eligible ground unit or Attack Helicopter up to its own formation size, except Armoured units.

For example, a Size 3 Strategic Airlift Aircraft can transport a Size 3, Size 2, or Size 1 eligible ground unit.

Strategic Airlift Aircraft have an operational range of 10 sectors from an airport or tanker and can use a tanker as an operational anchor.

Standard Airlift

Moving ground units between friendly airbases using Strategic Airlift Aircraft is a safe operation.

Units transported using standard airlift are not subject to suppression, formation-size reduction, or destruction during the transport.

Airdropping

Strategic Airlift Aircraft can conduct airdrop missions to deploy eligible ground units directly into friendly, contested, or enemy-controlled sectors.

Airdrop operations carry additional risks compared with conventional airlift.

Airdrop Mishaps

There is a 20% chance that a unit deployed by airdrop is reduced to its next smaller formation size.

This represents operational errors, difficult landing conditions, and other logistical mishaps associated with deploying forces directly into an operational area.

The Strategic Airlift Aircraft itself is not destroyed by the airdrop, even if the transported unit is damaged or eliminated.

Anti-Air and Strategic Airlift

Strategic Airlift Aircraft remain vulnerable to enemy air defence while conducting operations.

If the target sector of an airdrop is located within an enemy Air Defence Zone, standard Anti-Air rules apply if the Strategic Airlift Aircraft is engaged.

Air Superiority

Air superiority depends on more than the number of aircraft available to a nation.

Commanders must coordinate aircraft with airports, tankers, AWACS, Anti-Air systems, and other supporting forces while managing operational range and enemy airspace.

Fighters can engage hostile aircraft, specialised aircraft can support friendly operations, and stealth aircraft can penetrate defended areas with reduced exposure to enemy air defences.

Maintaining effective aerial operations therefore requires careful planning of both combat forces and the infrastructure that supports them.
