Submarine Warfare

Submarine Warfare encompasses both submarine operations and Anti-Submarine Warfare (ASW). Submarines use stealth and surprise to threaten enemy naval forces, while specialised units are required to detect, engage, and destroy them.

Submarines are high-risk, high-reward naval units. Their ability to potentially destroy enemy naval formations in a single successful attack makes them extremely dangerous, but their specialised combat rules and limited targets make their effective use dependent on careful positioning and timing.

Submarine Operations

Submarines can operate independently of conventional naval formations and conduct attacks against enemy naval units.

A submarine may perform only one action per turn. A submarine cannot attack and defend during the same turn.

Submarines do not require Supply Ships to operate and are not affected by the standard naval supply rules.

Submarine Combat

Submarine attacks use a specialised resolution system rather than the standard naval opposed-roll system.

When a submarine attacks an enemy naval unit, there is a 25% chance that the attack fails.

If the attack fails, the target takes no damage.

If the attack succeeds, the targeted naval unit is immediately destroyed.

This makes submarines a glass-cannon unit capable of dramatically changing a naval engagement when used effectively.

Anti-Submarine Warfare

Submarines can only be engaged and destroyed by specialised Anti-Submarine Warfare (ASW) units.

The following units are capable of engaging submarines:

Destroyer Surface combatants specialised for detecting and engaging enemy submarines. Submarine Submarines can engage enemy submarines during underwater combat. Marine Patrol Aircraft Aircraft specialised for detecting and attacking enemy submarines.

Submarine Evasion

Whenever a submarine is attacked, it has a 50% chance to evade the attack completely, regardless of the attacking unit's strength or combat roll.

If the submarine successfully evades, the attack has no effect. If the submarine fails to evade, the attack proceeds normally.

Marine Patrol Aircraft can attack submarines but cannot be attacked by submarines in return, as they are aircraft.

Destroyers

Destroyers are specialised surface combatants capable of detecting and engaging enemy submarines.

Destroyers provide an important defensive capability against submarine threats and are particularly valuable when operating alongside vulnerable surface formations.

Submarine vs Submarine

Submarines can engage other enemy submarines.

When a submarine attacks another submarine, the defending submarine first receives its normal 50% evasion chance.

If the defending submarine fails to evade, the attacking submarine's normal attack rules are then applied.

Marine Patrol Aircraft

Marine Patrol Aircraft (MPA) provide an aerial Anti-Submarine Warfare capability.

MPA can detect and attack enemy submarines but cannot be attacked by submarines in return, as they are aircraft rather than naval units.

Submarines therefore cannot directly engage MPA using their specialised submarine attack.

Submarine Supply

Submarines do not require Supply Ships and are not affected by the standard naval supply shortage rules.

This allows submarines to operate independently of surface fleets and supply ships, supporting their role as stealth assets capable of operating away from the main fleet.

Strategic Role

Submarines are designed to threaten valuable enemy naval assets and force opponents to dedicate resources to Anti-Submarine Warfare.

Their ability to instantly destroy a target on a successful attack makes them particularly dangerous against expensive naval formations.

However, their effectiveness depends on avoiding specialised ASW units. Once detected and engaged, their 50% evasion chance provides protection, but does not guarantee survival.

Effective submarine warfare therefore requires careful positioning, timing, and selection of targets.
