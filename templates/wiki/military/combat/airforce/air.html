Air Combat

Air Combat governs engagements between aircraft and the specialised systems that support aerial warfare. Aircraft operate across large areas of the battlefield and must manage operational range, contested airspace, enemy aircraft, and ground-based air defences.

Successful air operations depend on more than aircraft strength. Commanders must consider operational anchors, aircraft range, air defence coverage, formation size, and supporting assets when planning aerial operations.

Airports and Operational Range

Airports serve as the primary bases and anchor points for aircraft operations. Each aircraft may only operate within its defined movement range, measured in sectors from a friendly airport or tanker acting as an anchor point.

Aircraft cannot operate beyond their maximum range from a valid friendly anchor point. Strategically positioned airports and tankers are therefore critical to extending and maintaining air operations.

Example Operational Limits

Aircraft Maximum Range
Multi-Role Fighter (MRF) 4 sectors
Bomber 10 sectors
Helicopter 3 sectors

Tankers as Anchor Points

Aerial refuelling aircraft (Tankers) function as mobile anchor points for friendly aircraft. Aircraft may operate up to their movement range from a tanker in the same way they operate from an airport.

Tankers allow aircraft to remain within operational limits while conducting missions far from fixed airbases.

Tankers and Helicopters

Tankers and helicopters cannot operate from another tanker. Both must use an airport as their base of operations.

This prevents chains of mobile anchor points and ensures operational ranges remain limited to valid bases.

Loss of an Airport or Tanker

If a friendly airport or tanker acting as an anchor point is destroyed, aircraft relying on that anchor point must determine whether another friendly airport or tanker is within their operational range.

If an aircraft is not within range of another valid anchor point, it is lost and considered destroyed.

Contested Airspace

Aircraft may encounter enemy air units when entering sectors containing hostile aircraft.

When entering a contested sector, an air unit may either engage the enemy or bypass the sector.

Bypassing a contested sector costs 2 Movement Points. Engaging the enemy costs 1 Movement Point and represents direct combat with hostile air units occupying the sector.

The choice between engagement and bypassing allows commanders to decide whether to commit aircraft to aerial combat or continue toward their operational objective.

Aircraft Damage

Aircraft use formation size to represent the overall strength of an aerial formation.

When an air unit is hit, its formation size is immediately reduced by one level.

For example, a formation may be reduced from Wing to Squadron, or from Squadron to Flight. Reducing the formation represents aircraft losses and a corresponding reduction in the unit's combat effectiveness.

Aircraft Destruction

If an air unit is already at its smallest formation size and is hit again, the unit is permanently destroyed and removed from play.

This destruction rule applies regardless of the source of the damage.

Anti-Air Defence

Anti-Air units provide ground-based protection against hostile aircraft. They establish Air Defence Zones around their position and automatically engage enemy aircraft that enter or operate within their coverage.

An Anti-Air unit controls a one-sector radius around itself. Any enemy air unit entering or operating within this area is automatically engaged by the Anti-Air unit.

Rotary Warfare

Rotary-wing aircraft provide specialised battlefield support, primarily through direct close air support and anti-armour operations against enemy ground forces.

AWACS

AWACS (Airborne Warning and Control System) units provide airborne command and control, enhancing the effectiveness of friendly air operations across a wide area.

AWACS Coverage

An AWACS unit supports all friendly air units within a 2-sector radius.

Friendly air units within this coverage receive a +50% bonus to Strength and Defence, representing improved situational awareness, coordination, and targeting.

AWACS bonuses do not stack. If multiple AWACS units cover the same air unit, only one +50% bonus is applied.

Protection of AWACS

AWACS units are high-value assets and should be protected from enemy attack. Their loss can significantly reduce the effectiveness of friendly air operations and control of contested airspace.

AWACS Learn about AWACS units and their role in supporting friendly air forces.

Stealth Aircraft

Stealth aircraft are specialised aerial assets designed to operate with a reduced probability of detection and engagement by enemy air defence systems.

Stealth aircraft are twice as effective as non-stealth aircraft in terms of both Strength and Defence.

Their reduced detectability also makes them significantly more resistant to Anti-Air systems.

Anti-Air attacks against stealth aircraft have only a 20% chance of successfully hitting the aircraft, compared with an 80% chance against non-stealth aircraft.

Strategic Airlift

Strategic Airlift Aircraft provide rapid deployment of eligible ground forces across long distances. They can transport ground units between friendly airbases or conduct airdrop operations into operational areas.

Strategic Airlift Aircraft have an operational range of 10 sectors from an airport or tanker and can use a tanker as an operational anchor.

Strategic Airlift Learn about aircraft transport, airdrops, transport limitations, mishaps, and Anti-Air interactions.

Air Superiority

Air superiority depends on more than the number of aircraft available to a nation.

Commanders must coordinate aircraft with airports, tankers, AWACS, Anti-Air systems, and other supporting forces while managing operational range and enemy airspace.

Fighters can engage hostile aircraft, specialised aircraft can support friendly operations, and stealth aircraft can penetrate defended areas with reduced exposure to enemy air defences.

Maintaining effective aerial operations therefore requires careful planning of both combat forces and the infrastructure that supports them.
